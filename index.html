Know your machine

Understanding Your Washing Machine

There are three types on the market — and they are not the same. Know yours before you do anything else.

Front loader

Uses a pull-out tray that auto-doses each part of the cycle. Easiest to use, nightmare to keep mold-free.

💡 Leave the door open after every wash. Wipe the rubber seal. You will regret it if you don't.

Top loader — agitator

The center column "agitates" your clothes using friction. Don't overstuff it or nothing gets clean.

💡 Fill to 75% max. Overfilling = clothes flopping around without actually getting clean.

Top loader — impeller

A ridged plate at the bottom rotates clothes. Requires a very specific loading technique.

💡 Load clothes in a donut shape — hole in the middle. I know it sounds weird. Trust the donut.

The universal 75% rule

75% clothes
25% air ←

Quick test: make a fist above your clothes. If your knuckles touch the top of the drum, you're about right.

Machine care

Maintaining Your Machine

Clothes cannot get clean in a dirty washer. This is not up for debate. Clean it every ~90 loads — roughly once or twice a quarter.

"SCRUD" is a real word for the black gunky dots that appear when you finally clean a neglected machine. You're welcome for that knowledge.

🍋 Regular maintenance

Add ½ cup of pure citric acid powder to the drum and run the longest, hottest cycle. Repeat until the water runs clean. Cheap, effective, deeply satisfying.

🦠 If you have buildup / SCRUD

Run several citric acid cycles until the water clears. Wipe away any gross black SCRUD as it comes loose. Clean your detergent drawer and lint trap with an antibacterial like Microban. Google your specific machine model to find the filter.

Water science (yes, really)

Understanding Water Hardness

Hard water is quietly sabotaging your laundry and your machine — and most people have no idea. Look up your level in your city's municipal water report — a quick Google will find it. On well water? Test it with the API Fish Kit — you almost certainly have hard water.

Hard water doesn't just make cleaning harder. It actively works against your detergent. Your water is out here undermining you and you didn't even know.

Hard water is full of dissolved calcium and magnesium minerals — invisible, irritating, and working against you on two fronts. They make soil and stains harder to lift out of fabric, and they react with your detergent and reduce how well it works. So you're using more product, getting worse results, and blaming yourself. It's not you. It's the water.

They also make rinsing genuinely difficult. Detergent leaves wash water highly alkaline, and alkaline water doesn't rinse out cleanly — especially with minerals helping everything cling to the fabric. A small amount of acid in the final rinse neutralizes that alkalinity (this is called "souring" the rinse) so residue actually lets go. That is the entire job of citric acid in the softener slot. Not softening. Not fragrance. Just: finishing the rinse correctly.

Two words you have not heard since high school

pH & Alkalinity — Why It Matters

Water hardness tells you about minerals. pH tells you about chemistry. Both affect how well your detergent cleans — and they work together in ways worth understanding.

No, you don't need a chemistry degree. You just need to know that pH is a scale from 0 to 14, and that your laundry wants to be in a very specific part of it.

Tap water ~7
Detergent ~10
Citric acid ~3
0 — Acid357 — Neutral91114 — Alkaline

🍋 Acidic (0–6)

Rinse cycle territory. After a wash cycle your water is highly alkaline — and alkaline water rinses poorly, leaving detergent residue and mineral deposits clinging to fabric. Adding citric acid to the final rinse "sours" or neutralizes that alkalinity, so everything can actually release and wash away. This is especially critical with hard water, where the alkalinity problem is even more pronounced. It's not about scenting or softening — it's about completing the rinse properly.

😐 Neutral (7)

Plain tap water sits here. Neutral water alone doesn't clean much — it lacks the charge to lift oils and soils off fabric. This is why you need detergent. Soft water is close to neutral, which is actually a good starting point for detergent to work from.

🫧 Alkaline (8–14)

Wash cycle territory. Most detergents raise wash water to pH 9–11. This alkaline environment is what allows surfactants to break the bond between soil and fabric. The higher the pH, the more aggressive the clean — which is why hard water (already alkaline) can actually boost detergent, up to a point.

💡 The one-two punch: Alkaline wash → acidic rinse. Your detergent raises the pH to clean. Your citric acid rinse brings it back down, neutralizing residue and leaving fabric at a healthy ~5–6. That's the full chemistry cycle in one sentence.

When your water is working against you

Treating Hard Water — In Depth

Hard water minerals (calcium and magnesium) react with detergent and steal its cleaning power before it can reach your clothes. Here's exactly what to do about it.

When hard water minerals mix with detergent surfactants, they form a soap scum compound — literally insoluble deposits that never fully rinse out. Over time this builds up inside fabric fibers, leaving clothes stiff, dingy, and increasingly smelly even after washing. It also slowly coats your machine drum, pipes, and heating element.

The fix is sequestering agents — compounds that grab onto the calcium and magnesium ions before they can sabotage your detergent, holding them harmlessly in suspension so everything can rinse away.

★ Best pick
Washing Soda

Sodium carbonate. Raises pH and softens water simultaneously — the most efficient choice. Dose scales with your hardness level. Use more in the drum, not the drawer. Affordable and widely available.

Borax

Effective softener but works differently than washing soda — it buffers pH rather than raising it. Better for moderately hard water. Some moms avoid it around young kids; your call. Don't use both borax and washing soda together — they cancel out.

Citric Acid

Dissolves mineral buildup via acidic chelation. Works differently from washing soda — it's descaling, not water softening. Pair with washing soda: soda handles the wash, citric acid handles the rinse. Do not mix them in the same slot.

TSP

Trisodium phosphate — extremely effective, especially for very hard water. Not eco-friendly (phosphates cause algal blooms). A last resort for very hard water situations, not a regular-use product. Check local regulations.

Where to add sequestering agents: Always directly into the drum before loading clothes. Never in the detergent drawer — they'll interact with your detergent during dispensing and lose effectiveness before hitting the water. Washing soda + detergent in the drum, citric acid in the softener slot. Those are their lanes.

🚨 Signs your hard water treatment isn't working (yet)

Clothes feel stiff or crunchy after washing
White or grey residue on dark fabrics
Clothes smell musty even when "clean"
Towels become less absorbent over time
Scale buildup visible inside drum or door glass
Detergent isn't sudsing even with normal doses

If you're seeing these, increase your washing soda dose and run a Spa Day on your most affected clothes. The buildup took months to accumulate — give it a few washes to fully clear.

Detergent deep dive

Types of Detergents, Enzymes & Boosters

Choosing your detergent is personal. Eczema kid? Nostalgic for Tide? We hear you. But let's talk about what's actually in there first.

Laundry sheets are trash and pods are for college freshmen. You're an adult now. Time to level up.

What are surfactants? Short for "surface-acting agents," surfactants are the workhorses of your detergent. They connect soils to water — even oily or sticky soils that would cling to fabric — so everything can rinse away. They work best when paired with enzymes.

💧 Liquid detergent

Great surfactant package but works better paired with an enzymatic powder booster. Max dose: roughly 4 tbsp. That combo gives you the full spectrum of cleaning power.

🧂 Powder detergent

The overachiever — surfactants, enzymes, AND oxygen bleach in one. Sweet spot is 2–3 heaping tbsp in the drum. Pair with a booster on extra dirty loads.

⚡ Boosters (OxiClean, BIZ, FEBU)

Boosters add extra enzymes, stain fighters, and water softeners to supercharge your detergent. Honestly, you can just buy a tub of sodium percarbonate (the active ingredient in all of them) and use that. I use FEBU for adult clothes and BIZ for the kids' stuff — because I'm not made of money.

Know the lingo

Laundry Buzzwords You Should Know

Terms that get thrown around a lot — here's what they actually mean.

OBAs Know this

Optical Brightening Agents. These can fade darks over time. Found in almost all powdered detergents and sodium percarbonate products. FEBU booster is OBA-free, and free & clear liquids don't contain them — which is one of the main reasons I prefer pairing a liquid detergent with a booster.

Anti-Redeposition Agents Want this

This stops dirt from jumping back onto your clothes mid-wash. Yes, that's a real thing. All detergents listed in this guide contain anti-redeposition agents. It's one of the reasons those cheap detergents above don't actually clean — your clothes come out looking clean until the dirty rinse water redeposits the grime. Gross.

Odor Rebloom 😤 The worst

When your clothes come out of the wash smelling perfectly fine — and then you put them on and immediately stink. Most commonly found in synthetic fabrics (looking at you, every single pair of workout leggings ever made). It happens because body oil and bacteria haven't been fully washed away, and "rebloom" a delightful odor the moment you start moving around in them. A detergent or booster with lipase can help prevent odor rebloom, as can a citric acid rinse aid to make sure all the dirt, debris, and bacteria are truly gone during the rinse cycle. Heads up: when you first start using a lipase-based detergent, your clothes may actually smell worse coming out of the washer — that's the enzyme doing its job and pulling up everything that was lurking deep in the fibers. Gross but good. Just keep washing and those smells will slowly fade away for good.

When it comes to stains — especially with kids — pretreatment is everything. Buy several bottles and keep them all over the house. Bathroom, kids' rooms, the car. Everywhere.

The moment the clothes come off, spray them generously, toss in the hamper, and forget about it. Stain treater can sit on fabric for up to 7 days. You're welcome.

Stain School

How to Get Out Every Stain Ever

Because "OxiClean it and pray" is my #1 strategy too — but sometimes we need a little extra TLC.

⚡ Act fast — heat sets it permanently
1

Cold water only. Rinse immediately from the back of the fabric to push the stain out. Never warm or hot — heat bonds blood protein to fabric fibers forever.

2

Apply OxiClean Max Force or any pretreater that contains protease — that's the enzyme that specifically breaks down blood and other proteins. Work it in gently and let it sit for at least 30 minutes.

3

Wash on cold with your normal detergent and add a booster (FEBU, BIZ, or OxiClean) to give stain removal an extra push. Check before drying — if the stain is still there, treat again. Do not put it in the dryer.

💡 I know — I preach warm or hot for everything. Blood is the exception. Think of it like a steak: the longer you cook it, the less rare it becomes. Warm or hot water will literally cook this stain into your clothes. Cold only. No exceptions.

Hydrogen peroxide works on fresh blood on white or light fabrics. Apply, watch it fizz, rinse cold. Don't use on colors — it will bleach them.
🕐 For old blood stains

Follow the same steps above — but let your pretreater sit on the stain for longer before washing. When it comes out of the wash, there's a good chance the stain will still be there. That's okay. We have a plan.

1

Fill a bucket or bowl with hot water and add powdered oxygen bleach — OxiClean, Kirkland Oxi Boost, pure sodium percarbonate, any of them work. Stir until fully dissolved before adding the garment.

2

Submerge the garment and soak overnight. The extended soak gives the oxygen bleach time to work on what the wash cycle couldn't shift.

3

Wash as normal on cold. Check before drying. If it's still there, repeat the whole soak — it will eventually come out.

🕐 Sooner is better, but dried grease is treatable
1

Blot — don't rub. Remove any solid residue with a spoon. Rubbing pushes the grease deeper into the fibers. You will regret rubbing.

2

Apply Dawn Powerwash directly to the stain and work it in with your fingers. Dawn is a degreaser — it's literally designed for this. Let it sit 10–15 minutes.

3

Follow with OxiClean Max Force on top of the Dawn. Let both sit together for another 30 minutes.

4

Wash on warm or hot with a lipase-containing detergent and booster. Lipase is what breaks down oils — this is its moment.

💡 For old dried grease stains: apply a generous amount of dish soap and let it sit overnight before treating. The longer it sits, the more the lipase can work.
🕐 Treat before washing — don't just throw it in
1

Brush off any dried grass before you do anything wet. Getting it dry first prevents you from grinding it further in.

2

Apply OxiClean Max Force or a protease-based pretreater directly to the stain. Grass stains are protein + chlorophyll — you need protease and pectate lyase working together.

3

Let it sit for 48 hours minimum. This is not a rinse-and-wash situation. Give the enzymes time to actually break down the stain.

4

Wash on warm with a full enzyme detergent. Check before drying. Repeat the treatment if needed — grass is stubborn but it does come out.

💡 White baseball pants getting grass stains? This is what Out White Bright is for. Soak the pants separately before washing the full uniform.
✋ Wait — treating mud wet makes it worse
1

Let it dry completely. This is the hardest part. Do not touch it, rub it, or try to rinse it while it's wet — you will spread it further into the fibers. Walk away.

2

Once fully dry, brush off as much as possible with a stiff brush or the edge of a spoon. You want to remove the bulk before introducing any liquid.

3

Apply OxiClean Max Force and let it sit for at least an hour. Mud is clay and mineral-based — a good enzyme detergent and some patience is all you need.

4

Wash on warm or hot with your normal detergent and a booster. This one usually comes out in a single wash if you let it dry first.

💡 The most common mud mistake: panicking and rubbing it wet. Every time. Let. It. Dry.
⚡ Fresh ink is treatable. Set ink is a different story.
1

Place a clean cloth behind the stain so ink doesn't transfer through to the other side of the fabric.

2

For ballpoint ink: apply rubbing alcohol with a cotton ball and blot from the outside of the stain inward. The alcohol dissolves the oil-based ink. Repeat until it stops transferring.

3

For felt-tip or washable ink: cold water and dish soap first, then follow with OxiClean Max Force. These are water-based and much more forgiving.

4

Wash on cold or warm — not hot — and check before drying. Heat sets ink just like blood.

💡 Permanent marker on fabric is genuinely difficult. Rubbing alcohol will fade it but rarely removes it completely. Manage expectations and lean on the pretreater hard.
🕐 Treat before the stain sets in — don't let it sit in the hamper for a week
1

Those yellow pit stains aren't just sweat — they're a reaction between sweat and aluminum in antiperspirant. The fix is acid: mix equal parts white vinegar and water, apply, and let sit for 30 minutes.

2

Apply OxiClean Max Force or a sodium percarbonate paste on top and let it work for another 30–60 minutes. For really set-in yellowing, soak in hot water with OxiClean White Revive for several hours.

3

Wash on warm or hot with a lipase and protease detergent. Sweat is oils and proteins — you want both enzymes working.

4

For odor without visible staining — that's the gym clothes problem. See the Odor Rebloom section. The stain isn't on the surface, it's in the fibers.

💡 Prevention: switch to an aluminum-free deodorant and the yellow pit stain problem goes away entirely. Just saying.
🕐 Depends on the food — details below
1

Scrape off any solids first with a spoon — never rub. Then blot any wet residue with a clean cloth.

2

Identify what you're dealing with. Greasy food (butter, oil, salad dressing) → start with Dawn Powerwash. Tomato-based (pasta sauce, ketchup) → OxiClean Max Force straight away. Protein-based (egg, meat) → cold water first, then pretreater. Turmeric → sodium percarbonate soak in hot water, then sunlight.

3

Apply your pretreater and let it sit for 48 hours minimum. Food stains respond dramatically better with time.

4

Wash on warm or hot with a full enzyme detergent and booster. Check before drying — food stains that have been dried in are significantly harder to shift.

💡 Turmeric is in a category of its own. Sodium percarbonate soak + direct sunlight is the most effective treatment. Sunlight acts as a natural bleaching agent on turmeric specifically — leave the damp garment outside and let the sun do its thing.
⚡ Cold water only — heat is chocolate's best friend (and yours worst enemy)
1

Let it dry or harden first if it's fresh melted chocolate. Trying to treat it while it's still liquid smears it further. Give it a few minutes, then gently scrape off the solid with a spoon.

2

Rinse from the back with cold water to push the stain out rather than deeper in. Chocolate is a triple threat — it's protein, fat, AND tannin — so you're fighting on three fronts.

3

Apply Dawn Powerwash first to tackle the fat, work it in gently, then follow immediately with OxiClean Max Force for the protein and tannin. Let both sit together for at least an hour — longer if it's dried in.

4

Wash on cold or warm — never hot — with a full enzyme detergent. Protease for the protein, lipase for the fat. Check before drying. Repeat if needed.

💡 Milk chocolate is slightly easier than dark chocolate because it has more fat and less tannin. Dark chocolate on white fabric is the final boss. Be patient, be cold, and treat more than once if you need to.
⚡ Turmeric especially — do not let this dry if you can help it
1

Scrape off any solid residue immediately. Turmeric and spice stains are pigment-based — the longer they sit in the fiber, the deeper they bond. Speed matters more here than almost any other stain.

2

Apply dish soap or Dawn Powerwash immediately and work it in. Rinse with cold water. This won't remove it fully but it buys you time and lifts the surface layer before you move to the heavy treatment.

3

Make a sodium percarbonate paste — mix pure sodium percarbonate (or OxiClean) with just enough hot water to form a thick paste. Apply generously to the stain and leave it for several hours. This is the most effective dry treatment for turmeric.

4

Wash on warm with a full enzyme detergent and booster. Then — and this is the secret — lay the damp garment in direct sunlight. Sunlight is a natural bleaching agent that specifically targets turmeric pigment. Leave it until dry. Repeat the whole process if the stain is still visible.

💡 Turmeric is one of the few stains where the dryer is your actual enemy. Never tumble dry a turmeric-stained garment until the stain is completely gone — heat bonds the pigment permanently. The sun is your friend here. Your dryer is not.

Let's get washing

How to Actually Do a Load

You are never washing on cold again. Cold is dead to us. Warm or hot, every time — that's how enzymes and surfactants actually activate. Also: towels ONLY get washed with other towels. This is non-negotiable.

"But cold is better for the environment!" Cool. These are your clothes. Wash them on warm.

1

Add any powder boosters or water softeners directly into the drum first — before the clothes go in.

2

Load clothes to 75% capacity. Not 80%. Not "pretty full." 75%.

3

Add 4 tbsp of liquid detergent into your machine's detergent section. If you've softened your water correctly, this is the max you ever need. For powder, 2–3 heaping tbsp in the drum.

4

Add ¼ cup of fabric rinse (citric acid solution, Downy Rinse & Refresh, or homemade) into the softener section. If your machine doesn't have one, use a Downy ball tossed in the drum.

5

Select warm or hot and use as many extra rinses as your machine allows. Yes, really. Three rinses. I know. Just do it.

6

Walk away for 2 hours. Come back to the cleanest laundry of your entire life.

The rinse cycle secret

Why Citric Acid in the Rinse?

If you're using vinegar in your rinse cycle, you're not using nearly enough — it takes 2–3 cups of household cleaning vinegar to actually work. Citric acid is more concentrated, cheaper, and your clothes won't smell like a salad.

🧫

Neutralizes detergent

Most detergents are alkaline. Citric acid neutralizes the residue, eliminating that stiff, scratchy feeling — even if you accidentally overdosed.

💎

Fights hard water minerals

Binds to calcium and magnesium so they rinse away instead of leaving a chalky film on your clothes or inside your machine.

🌿

Softens without coating

Fabric softener leaves a waxy layer. Citric acid softens by removing buildup. Nothing added, just residue gone.

⚙️

Descales your machine

Regular use prevents mineral scale buildup inside the drum and hoses over time. Your machine will silently thank you.

The finishing touch

Keeping Towels & Sheets Soft and Bright

If your towels feel scratchy, it's almost always detergent buildup — too much product coats the fibers so they can't absorb water properly. Here's the fix.

Towels ONLY get washed with other towels. This is not a suggestion. This is a way of life.

🔄 Towel rehab — stripping the buildup
1️⃣

Add ½ cup of citric acid directly to the drum.

2️⃣

Load towels and wash on HOT, heavy soil, with extra rinses. Repeat until you see no more suds — that's all the old detergent coming out. Then promise me you won't overdose your detergent ever again.

3️⃣

Dry on a low/delicate heat setting. Towels are a looped fabric — high heat destroys them. Pull out when barely damp and fold as normal.

🤍 Keeping white towels white

Always hot, always extra rinses, slightly less detergent (you're clean when you use a towel — there's not much dirt for detergent to cling to). If they start going dingy, soak in a hot bath with OxiClean White Revive or pure sodium percarbonate for a few hours, then wash and dry as normal.

🛏 White sheets — same rules, extra step

Follow the same routine as white towels, but always pretreat any stains first. I have a large golden retriever who sleeps in our bed every single night and our cotton sheets are still a lovely bright white. The routine works.

Stains and stink are basically a sponsorship deal when you have athletes. Here's how to handle the specific chaos each sport brings.

Whoever decided that white pants belong on a dirt field is literally insane. Anyway. Here's how to undo the damage.

1

Everything into a boiling hot soak — dissolve 1½ cups of powdered detergent in the hot water and submerge jerseys, socks, the whole uniform. Soak for at least 1 hour.

2

Pants get their own soak (separate step, just the pants). Hot water + ½ cup of Out White Bright. Soak for at least 1 hour.

3

Drain the gross soak water and throw everything in the washer. Warm or hot, good detergent, multiple rinses. Add 1–2 tbsp of sodium percarbonate (BIZ, FEBU, or OxiClean) to the drum if you want to go all in.

Hockey moms, I am about to become one of you. I have done an unhinged amount of research in preparation. Here's what I've learned.

1

Anything that can go in the washer, wash it. Jerseys, socks, base layers — wash as normal and air dry.

2

Everything else must air out after every single practice. Don't let gear sit in a bag. Ever. Open it up, spread it out, let it breathe.

3

Spray non-washable gear with hypochlorous acid to kill bacteria and odor. Buy an enormous jug and a spray bottle and just go to town.

💡 Hypochlorous acid is safe, non-toxic, and genuinely effective — the same thing used in wound care. A large jug costs almost nothing and lasts forever. Keep it in your gear bag.

The big guns

Ammonia — When to Call in the Cavalry

Not an everyday product. But if your husband comes home smelling like a garage, this is your friend.

Mechanics' wives, farmers' wives, anyone married to a person who works with their hands — this one's for you. You're welcome.

Ammonia is an alkaline cleaner with a particular talent for cutting through grease, oil, and heavy grime that regular detergent struggles to shift. It's been used in laundry for decades — it just fell out of fashion when detergents got better. On extremely soiled loads, it still earns its keep.

The key word is extremely. This is not something you're reaching for on a Tuesday with a regular load. It's for the mechanic's work clothes. The farm gear. The mystery-stained situation you've already washed twice and it's only getting worse. Used correctly, it's effective and safe. Used casually on everything, it's unnecessary and will shorten the life of your fabrics.

✅ What it's good for

Heavy grease and oil stains, embedded grime, work clothes that smell like a job site, anything where detergent alone isn't making a dent. Pairs especially well with a Spa Day soak — add it right before you transfer clothes to the machine as your final step.

🚫 The one rule you cannot break

Never mix ammonia with bleach — or with any product containing bleach. This produces toxic chloramine gas. It is not a "don't do this at home" warning. It is a do-not-do-this-anywhere warning. Keep them completely separate, always.

💡 How to use it: Pour about 1 cup of plain liquid ammonia into the liquid bleach slot in your machine drawer — that's exactly what that slot is for. On a top loader, you can also add it directly to the drum after the wash water has filled, before the cycle starts. Wash on warm or hot with an extra rinse. And while we're here: you shouldn't be putting liquid bleach in that slot anyway. We have better options. Consider it ammonia's slot now.

What to buy

Plain, unscented household ammonia — the kind with no added surfactants or cleaning agents. It's usually labeled "clear ammonia" or "pure ammonia." The scented versions and multipurpose formulas have other stuff in them you don't need in your laundry. It costs almost nothing and a bottle lasts forever because you're barely using it.

The resurrection

Spa Day 🧖‍♀️

How to give your stinky, dirty, dead-to-you clothes a second chance at life.

This is not regular laundry. This is a full intervention. Commit to the process.

What is Spa Day? A hot soak treatment for clothes that regular washing just isn't cutting — think gym clothes with permanent stink, thrift finds, heavily soiled items, or anything you've given up on. The extended soak lets enzymes and surfactants work deep into the fibers in a way a normal wash cycle simply can't.

Gather your tools

Cooler or plastic storage bin with lid
Wooden or plastic cooking spoon
1-cup measuring cup
Old towel (to weigh clothes down)
Tide Original Powder — easiest option
Cleaning gloves (for sensitive skin)
Fine mesh kitchen strainer
Bucket or smaller bin for transfer
Liquid ammonia (odor nuclear option)
Citric acid (for the rehab rinse)
1

Set up your bin in the bathtub. Fill it ⅓ to ½ with hot tap water.

2

Add 1½ cups of Tide Original Powder per ~6 gallons of water. Stir until all granules are fully dissolved — this takes a few minutes. Use the strainer to fish out any stubborn granules. Undissolved powder can leave spots.

3

Add your clothes. Make sure everything is fully submerged. Lay the old towel on top to keep it all under the water. Put the lid on.

4

Wait 8–12 hours. Overnight works great. Set a reminder alarm so you don't forget about your laundry soup.

5

Drain — do not wring or squeeze. While it drains, prep your washing machine: detergent in the drawer, citric acid in the softener slot, settings pre-selected.

6

Transfer clothes to the machine. If using ammonia, pour 1 cup directly onto the soaked clothes, then immediately close the door and press start. Speed matters here — ammonia should be your very last step.

7

Wash on warm or hot, heavy soil, with an extra rinse. Normal cycles are fine for most fabrics.

8

Dry as normal — delicates hang to dry, everything else on low/delicate heat. Don't dry on hot if you're still working on a stain.

💜 Long-term maintenance: incorporate a lipase detergent or booster into your regular routine to prevent buildup from coming back. Citric acid in every rinse keeps things fresh.

✦ The TLDR

For when you just need to start doing laundry correctly right now. You've got kids. I get it.

  • 1

    Know your water hardness → add the right softener to the drum before loading.

  • 2

    Fill to 75% only. Never overfill. Not even close.

  • 3

    4 tbsp liquid detergent + a booster (FEBU for adults, BIZ for kids) into the drum.

  • 4

    ¼ cup citric acid rinse in the softener slot. No fabric softener. Ever.

  • 5

    Warm or hot, as many rinses as your machine allows. Cold is not an option.

  • 6

    Pretreat every stain with OxiClean Max Force the moment it happens. Let it sit 48h minimum.

  • 7

    Clean your machine with ½ cup citric acid every 90 loads. Don't skip this.

  • 8

    Towels only with towels. Hot. Low heat dry. Take out slightly damp. That's it.

Know your machine

Understanding Your Washing Machine

There are three types on the market — and they are not the same. Know yours before you do anything else. Then there's one more label that matters: HE vs. non-HE (more on that below).

Front loader

Uses a pull-out tray that auto-doses each part of the cycle. Easiest to use, nightmare to keep mold-free.

💡 Leave the door open after every wash. Wipe the rubber seal. You will regret it if you don't.

Top loader — agitator

The center column "agitates" your clothes using friction. Don't overstuff it or nothing gets clean.

💡 Fill to 75% max. Overfilling = clothes flopping around without actually getting clean.

Top loader — impeller

A ridged plate at the bottom rotates clothes. Requires a very specific loading technique.

💡 Load clothes in a donut shape — hole in the middle. I know it sounds weird. Trust the donut.

The universal 75% rule

25% air ←

Quick test: make a fist above your clothes. If your knuckles touch the top of the drum, you're about right.

HE vs. Non-HE — and why it changes how you dose

Cutting across all three machine types above is one more label that matters: HE (High Efficiency) vs. non-HE. It's not marketing fluff — it changes how much detergent you should actually use, and getting it wrong is one of the most common laundry mistakes out there.

💧 HE (High Efficiency)

Uses dramatically less water — an ENERGY STAR HE machine averages about 14 gallons per load, and the most efficient front loaders can clean with as little as 7 gallons. Instead of soaking your clothes in a full tub, HE machines tumble or spin them through a shallow pool with high-pressure sprays. Look for the HE symbol on the machine. Most front loaders and modern top loaders are HE.

🌊 Non-HE (Traditional)

The older style — a top loader with an agitator that fills the whole tub and soaks your clothes the entire cycle. They're water guzzlers: an older agitator machine can use about 40 gallons per load, versus around 20 for a standard machine. If your machine fills all the way up with water every time, it's almost certainly non-HE.

⚠️ The dosing trap nobody warns you about: HE machines need way less detergent than the bottle or cup tells you. Most powder detergents say to fill to line 5 on the cup for an HE machine — that is WAY too much. 2–3 heaping tablespoons is plenty. Because HE machines use so little water, regular dosing means there's not enough water to rinse it all out. Too much detergent leaves behind residue and leads to longer cycle times — plus buildup, funk, and that "why are my clothes still kind of stiff" feeling. Less is genuinely more here.
💡 And do NOT add extra water to an HE machine. I know the shallow puddle looks wrong. It's not. Consumer Reports found that adding extra water actually makes clothes come out dingier — the cleaning comes from clothes rubbing against each other, not from swimming in a full tub. Trust the machine. (See? Trust the donut, trust the puddle. I'm consistent.)

Maintaining Your Machine

Clothes cannot get clean in a dirty washer. Clean it once a month — no clothes, no detergent, just the hottest longest cycle your machine has. That's it. That's the rule.

"SCRUD" is a real word for the dark, waxy buildup that forms when detergent residue and fabric softener bake onto your drum over time. You can't always see it. It's getting on your clothes. Clean your machine.

🍋 The citric acid method

Add ½ cup of pure citric acid powder directly to the drum and run the longest, hottest cycle. Repeat until the water runs clean. Cheap, effective, and deeply satisfying. This is also what you're doing during Spa Day machine maintenance.

🦠 If you have buildup / SCRUD

Run several citric acid cycles until the water clears, wiping away any black SCRUD as it comes loose. Clean your detergent drawer and lint trap with an antibacterial like Microban. Google your specific machine model to find the filter — yes, there's a filter. Yes, you should clean it.

🧹 Clean the filter & drawer

Use your machine manual to find your filter, clean it out, and drain the extra water sitting in there (have a towel ready — it's always more than you expect). Then pull out your detergent drawer and give it a good wipe down to prevent mold and buildup. Do this regularly and your machine will thank you.

🏊 Front loader pro tip: My competitive swimmer days taught me this one — when water got stuck in my ear, my mom used isopropyl alcohol drops to help it evaporate faster. Same theory for your front loader: keep a spray bottle of isopropyl alcohol on the machine, and after your last load, give the rubber door seal a quick spritz and wipe. The alcohol helps the trapped water evaporate, so you get way less mildew and funk.

Water science (yes, really)

Understanding Water Hardness

Hard water is quietly sabotaging your laundry and your machine — and most people have no idea. Look up your level in your city's municipal water report — a quick Google will find it. Heads up: some reports list hardness in gpg (grains per gallon) instead of ppm — multiply by 17.1 to convert. On well water? Test it with the API Fish Kit — yes, the aquarium one; the GH test is your water hardness — because you almost certainly have hard water.

Hard water doesn't just make cleaning harder. It actively works against your detergent. Your water is out here undermining you and you didn't even know.

Hard water is full of dissolved calcium and magnesium minerals — invisible, irritating, and working against you on two fronts. They make soil and stains harder to lift out of fabric, and they react with your detergent and reduce how well it works. So you're using more product, getting worse results, and blaming yourself. It's not you. It's the water.

They also make rinsing genuinely difficult. Detergent leaves wash water highly alkaline, and alkaline water doesn't rinse out cleanly — especially with minerals helping everything cling to the fabric. A small amount of acid in the final rinse neutralizes that alkalinity (this is called "souring" the rinse) so residue actually lets go. That is the entire job of citric acid in the softener slot. Not softening. Not fragrance. Just: finishing the rinse correctly.

Untreated hard water means literally nothing — zero bubbles, plus stiff, dingy clothes. Crunchy towels and a bone-dry window? That's your water talking, not your dose.

Two words you have not heard since high school

pH & Alkalinity — Why It Matters

Water hardness tells you about minerals. pH tells you about chemistry. Both affect how well your detergent cleans — and they work together in ways that are actually kind of fascinating once you stop being scared of them.

No, you don't need a chemistry degree. You just need to know that pH is a scale from 0 to 14, and that your laundry wants to live at two very specific points on it — one for washing, one for rinsing. That's it. That's the whole class.

🍋 Acidic (0–6)

Rinse cycle territory. After washing, your water is highly alkaline — and alkaline water rinses terribly, leaving detergent residue and minerals stuck to everything. Citric acid in the final rinse "sours" that alkalinity so it all actually lets go. Especially important with hard water. Not about scent. Not about softening. Just: finishing the job properly.

😐 Neutral (7)

Plain tap water lives here. Perfectly fine for drinking. Basically useless for laundry on its own — it doesn't have the charge to lift oils and soils off fabric. This is why detergent exists. Soft water starts close to neutral, which is actually a great base to work from.

🫧 Alkaline (8–14)

Wash cycle territory. Detergent pushes wash water up to pH 9–11, which is what allows surfactants to actually break the bond between dirt and fabric. The more alkaline, the more aggressive the clean. Hard water is already alkaline — which sounds helpful, until the minerals start causing their own problems.

💡 The one-two punch: Alkaline wash → acidic rinse. Your detergent raises the pH to clean. Your citric acid rinse brings it back down, neutralizing residue and leaving fabric at a healthy ~5–6. That's the full chemistry cycle in one sentence.

When your water is working against you

Treating Hard Water — In Depth

Hard water minerals (calcium and magnesium) react with detergent and steal its cleaning power before it can reach your clothes. Rude. Here's exactly what to do about it.

There is a whole category of products specifically designed to deal with your water so your detergent doesn't have to. They're cheap. They work. You should be using them.

When hard water minerals meet detergent surfactants, they form insoluble soap scum deposits that never fully rinse out — building up in fabric fibers over time and leaving clothes stiff, dingy, and increasingly smelly no matter how much detergent you use. It also coats your machine drum and heating element. Nothing about this is your fault. It's just chemistry being annoying.

The fix is sequestering agents — compounds that grab onto the calcium and magnesium ions before they can cause chaos, holding them harmlessly in suspension so everything actually rinses away clean.

★ Best pick
Washing Soda

Sodium carbonate. Raises pH and softens water simultaneously — the most efficient choice. Dose scales with your hardness level. Use more in the drum, not the drawer. Affordable and widely available.

Borax

Effective softener but works differently than washing soda — it buffers pH rather than raising it. Better for moderately hard water. Some moms avoid it around young kids; your call. And yes — borax and washing soda play beautifully together. My homemade softener mix is exactly that combo, and it's in every load I run.

Sodium Citrate

A chelating agent — it binds to calcium and magnesium minerals and neutralizes them, rather than removing them the way washing soda does. Works a little differently but is very effective in hard water. A great addition to your routine if washing soda alone isn't cutting it.

TSP

Trisodium phosphate — extremely effective, especially for very hard water. Not eco-friendly (phosphates cause algal blooms). A last resort for very hard water situations, not a regular-use product. Check local regulations.

Where to add these: Always straight into the drum before the clothes go in. Never the detergent drawer — they'll react with your detergent on the way out and lose their punch before they even hit the water. Softeners and detergent in the drum, your rinse aid in the softener slot. Everybody stays in their lane.

Detergent deep dive

Types of Detergents, Enzymes & Boosters

Choosing your detergent is personal. Eczema kid? Nostalgic for Tide? We hear you. But let's talk about what's actually in there first.

Laundry sheets are trash and pods are for college freshmen. You're an adult now. Time to level up.

What are surfactants? Short for "surface-acting agents," surfactants are the workhorses of your detergent. They connect soils to water — even oily or sticky soils that would cling to fabric — so everything can rinse away. They work best when paired with enzymes.

💧 Liquid detergent

Great surfactant package but works better paired with an enzymatic powder booster. Max dose: roughly 4 tbsp. That combo gives you the full spectrum of cleaning power.

🧂 Powder detergent

The overachiever — surfactants, enzymes, AND oxygen bleach in one. Sweet spot is 2–3 heaping tbsp in the drum. Pair with a booster on extra dirty loads.

⚡ Boosters (OxiClean, BIZ, FEBU)

Boosters add extra enzymes, stain fighters, and water softeners to supercharge your detergent. The oxygen-based ones are built on sodium percarbonate — but the enzyme boosters (BIZ, FEBU, DadMode) add proteases and other enzymes on top, which plain sodium percarbonate doesn't have. I use FEBU for adult clothes and BIZ for the kids' stuff — because I'm not made of money.

Know the lingo

Laundry Buzzwords You Should Know

Terms that get thrown around a lot — here's what they actually mean.

OBAs Know this

Optical Brightening Agents. These can fade darks over time. Found in almost all powdered detergents and sodium percarbonate products. FEBU booster is OBA-free, and free & clear liquids don't contain them — which is one of the main reasons I prefer pairing a liquid detergent with a booster. Real talk: I have young kids and I am NOT in my "safe to wear white" era. I wear a lot of darks, ok? So I generally steer clear of OBAs, which is exactly why the Liquid Tide + FEBU combo is my go-to for adult clothes.

Anti-Redeposition Agents Want this

This stops dirt from jumping back onto your clothes mid-wash. Yes, that's a real thing. All detergents listed in this guide contain anti-redeposition agents. It's one of the reasons those cheap detergents above don't actually clean — your clothes come out looking clean until the dirty rinse water redeposits the grime. Gross.

Odor Rebloom 😤 The worst

When your clothes come out of the wash smelling perfectly fine — and then you put them on and immediately stink. Most commonly found in synthetic fabrics (looking at you, every single pair of workout leggings ever made). It happens because body oil and bacteria haven't been fully washed away, and "rebloom" a delightful odor the moment you start moving around in them. A detergent or booster with lipase can help prevent odor rebloom, as can a citric acid rinse aid to make sure all the dirt, debris, and bacteria are truly gone during the rinse cycle. Heads up: when you first start using a lipase-based detergent, your clothes may actually smell worse coming out of the washer — that's the enzyme doing its job and pulling up everything that was lurking deep in the fibers. Gross but good. Just keep washing and those smells will slowly fade away for good.

The MVP of the laundry room

Oxygen Bleach: Your Must-Have

If I could only keep one thing in my laundry room besides detergent, it would be oxygen bleach. It brightens, it lifts stains, it deodorizes, and it does it all without wrecking your colors or your fabric the way chlorine bleach does. Non-negotiable in my house.

Chlorine bleach is a sledgehammer. Oxygen bleach is a scalpel — it gets the job done without destroying everything around it.

What it actually is: Oxygen bleach is sodium percarbonate — a powder that, the moment it hits water, breaks down into hydrogen peroxide and soda ash (washing soda). The hydrogen peroxide is the workhorse: it releases oxygen that lifts stains and brightens fabric, while the soda ash gives your wash a gentle alkaline boost. That's the whole magic. It's the active oxygen-bleaching agent inside products like OxiClean and 365 Oxygen Whitener.

Why it's color-safe when chlorine isn't: Chlorine bleach chemically strips color and weakens fibers. Oxygen bleach works through active oxygen instead, so on most colored fabrics it lifts the stain without stripping the dye. (Always spot-test anyway — some dyes are sensitive, and it's not recommended for wool, silk, or anything with special finishes.)

🔥 How to activate it (this part matters)

Oxygen bleach needs warm or hot water to activate — the hotter the water, the faster and harder it works. Cold water barely wakes it up. Always dissolve the powder fully in hot water FIRST, before your clothes go in, so it's evenly distributed and you don't get concentrated spots on fabric. Then add your items.

⏳ Use it fresh

Once dissolved in water, oxygen bleach starts losing its power — it's most potent in the first several hours, then gradually fizzles out. So mix it when you're ready to use it, don't pre-mix a batch and let it sit for days. For a soak, get it in the water and get your items in while it's active.

🎯 When to reach for it

Set-in stains, dingy whites, yellowed fabric, funky towels, a whole load that needs a refresh, or a Spa Day ↓ soak. It's your go-to whenever a regular wash isn't cutting it and you need to lift something stubborn — especially tannin stains like coffee and wine.

💛 Why it's a must-have

One tub does the work of half a dozen specialty products. It boosts your detergent, rescues stained items you'd otherwise toss, keeps whites white, and it's gentle enough for regular use. Cheap, versatile, and endlessly useful — the definition of an MVP.

💡 Heads up on OBAs — read your label. Check the ingredients on your oxygen bleach of choice for brightening agents (like Fluorescent Brightener 71, one you'll often see). If your oxygen bleach contains one, it can fade your darks over time — so either use that one on whites and colors only, or choose an "all-purpose" oxygen bleach you can safely use on everything. Whatever works for your family routine and your budget. If you want to skip brighteners entirely, pure sodium percarbonate is just the oxygen bleach with nothing added.

The Importance of Pretreatment

When it comes to stains — especially with kids — pretreatment is everything. Buy several bottles and keep them all over the house. Bathroom, kids' rooms, the car. Everywhere.

The moment the clothes come off, spray them generously, toss in the hamper, and forget about it. Stain treater can sit on fabric for up to 7 days. You're welcome.

Stain happens→ Spray generously→ Toss in hamper→ Wait 48h–7 days→ Wash normally ✓

48 hours is the default for tough stains — it's not a law. Some stains in Stain School have their own timing (blood moves fast, grease is slow), so check the tab before you panic-wait two days on a nosebleed.

For especially stubborn stains (turmeric, paint, pasta sauce): re-apply pretreatment daily until wash day. Takes 2 minutes. Leave the item somewhere you'll see it. You will be amazed.

Stain School

How to Get Out Every Stain Ever

Because "OxiClean it and pray" is my #1 strategy too — but sometimes we need a little extra TLC.

⚡ Act fast — heat sets it permanently
1

Cold water only. Rinse immediately from the back of the fabric to push the stain out. Never warm or hot — heat bonds blood protein to fabric fibers forever.

2

Apply OxiClean Max Force or any pretreater that contains protease — that's the enzyme that specifically breaks down blood and other proteins. Work it in gently and let it sit for at least 30 minutes.

3

Wash on cold with your normal detergent and add a booster (FEBU, BIZ, or OxiClean) to give stain removal an extra push. Check before drying — if the stain is still there, treat again. Do not put it in the dryer.

💡 I know — I preach warm or hot for everything. Blood is the exception. Think of it like a steak: the longer you cook it, the less rare it becomes. Warm or hot water will literally cook this stain into your clothes. Cold only. No exceptions.

Hydrogen peroxide works on fresh blood on white or light fabrics. Apply, watch it fizz, rinse cold. Don't use on colors — it will bleach them.
🕐 For old blood stains

Follow the same steps above — but let your pretreater sit on the stain for longer before washing. When it comes out of the wash, there's a good chance the stain will still be there. That's okay. We have a plan.

1

Fill a bucket or bowl with hot water and add powdered oxygen bleach — OxiClean, Kirkland Oxi Boost, pure sodium percarbonate, any of them work. Stir until fully dissolved before adding the garment.

2

Submerge the garment and soak overnight. The extended soak gives the oxygen bleach time to work on what the wash cycle couldn't shift.

3

Wash as normal on cold. Check before drying. If it's still there, repeat the whole soak — it will eventually come out.

🕐 Sooner is better, but dried grease is treatable
1

Blot — don't rub. Remove any solid residue with a spoon. Rubbing pushes the grease deeper into the fibers. You will regret rubbing.

2

Apply Dawn Powerwash or blue Dawn directly to the stain and work it in with your fingers. Dawn is a degreaser — it's literally designed for this. Let it sit 10–15 minutes.

3

Follow with OxiClean Max Force on top of the Dawn. Let both sit together for 48 hours. Yes, two days. Grease is stubborn and the enzymes need time to work. Set a reminder and walk away.

4

Wash on warm or hot with a lipase-containing detergent and booster. Lipase is what breaks down oils — this is her moment. Let her work.

💡 For old dried grease stains: apply a generous amount of dish soap and let it sit overnight before treating. The longer it sits, the more the lipase can work.
🕐 Treat before washing — don't just throw it in
1

Brush off any dried grass before you do anything wet. Getting it dry first prevents you from grinding it further in.

2

Apply OxiClean Max Force or a protease-based pretreater directly to the stain. Grass stains are protein + chlorophyll — you need protease and pectate lyase working together.

3

Let it sit for 48 hours minimum. This is not a rinse-and-wash situation. Give the enzymes time to actually break down the stain.

4

Wash on warm with a full enzyme detergent. Check before drying. Repeat the treatment if needed — grass is stubborn but it does come out.

💡 White baseball pants getting grass stains? This is what Out White Bright is for. Soak the pants separately before washing the full uniform.
✋ Wait — treating mud wet makes it worse
1

Let it dry completely. This is the hardest part. Do not touch it, rub it, or try to rinse it while it's wet — you will spread it further into the fibers. Walk away.

2

Once fully dry, brush off as much as possible with a stiff brush or the edge of a spoon. You want to remove the bulk before introducing any liquid.

3

Apply OxiClean Max Force and let it sit for at least an hour. Mud is clay and mineral-based — a good enzyme detergent and some patience is all you need.

4

Wash on warm or hot with your normal detergent and a booster. This one usually comes out in a single wash if you let it dry first.

💡 The most common mud mistake: panicking and rubbing it wet. Every time. Let. It. Dry.
⚡ Fresh ink is treatable. Set ink is a different story. And "washable" markers — I use that word loosely.
1

Place a clean cloth behind the stain so ink doesn't transfer straight through to the other side. Basic but important.

2

For ballpoint ink: saturate with rubbing alcohol and blot — do not rub — from the outside of the stain inward. Keep blotting with fresh sections of cloth until it stops transferring. The alcohol dissolves the oil-based ink.

3

For felt-tip, marker, or "washable" ink (and kids f**king LOVE markers — dry erase, "washable," permanent, all of them, especially on fabric): your regular pretreater isn't going to cut it. Rubbing alcohol first, then OxiClean Max Force. For serious marker situations, call in Amodex or Carbona Stain Devil 3 — and follow those instructions VERY closely. These are not products to freestyle with.

4

Wash on cold or warm — not hot — and check before drying. Heat sets ink just like blood. If permanent marker is still visible after washing, repeat. It will fade. Manage expectations.

💡 "Washable" is doing a LOT of heavy lifting on those marker labels. Treat it like regular marker and you won't be disappointed.
🕐 Treat before the stain sets in — don't let it sit in the hamper for a week
1

Those yellow pit stains aren't just sweat — they're a reaction between sweat and aluminum in antiperspirant. The fix is acid: mix equal parts white vinegar and water, apply, and let sit for 30 minutes.

2

Apply OxiClean Max Force or a sodium percarbonate paste on top and let it work for another 30–60 minutes. For really set-in yellowing, soak in hot water with OxiClean White Revive for several hours.

3

Wash on warm or hot with a lipase and protease detergent. Sweat is oils and proteins — you want both enzymes working.

4

For odor without visible staining — that's the gym clothes problem. Please see Spa Day. ESPECIALLY if you are a reformed All Free and Clear user (read: All Free and Useless). The stain isn't on the surface, it's deep in the fibers, and a regular wash cycle isn't going to reach it.

💡 Prevention: switch to an aluminum-free deodorant and the yellow pit stain problem goes away entirely. Just saying.
🕐 Easier than you'd think — you probably already own the fix
1

Reach for micellar water. Makeup is easy, and odds are this is already sitting in your bathroom. Micellar water is the best first move — it's literally designed to lift makeup off skin, so it does the same on fabric.

2

Saturate the stain and blot over a rag. Lay the stained area over a clean cloth, soak it with micellar water, and blot — the makeup transfers down onto the rag. Keep going until you've pulled out the majority of it.

3

Pretreat with a stain remover that has protease AND lipase. Makeup is a combo of proteins, fats, and oils — protease handles the proteins, lipase handles the fats and oils. Puracy Stain Remover has both, which makes it the best pick here. Let it sit.

4

Wash on warm as normal. Check before drying, and repeat the micellar-water-and-pretreat step if any color is hanging on (looking at you, waterproof mascara and full-coverage foundation).

💡 Micellar water is the unsung hero here — keep a bottle near your laundry zone, not just your bathroom. Foundation on a collar, lipstick on a sleeve, concealer on a towel — it handles all of it.
🕐 Depends on the food — details below
1

Scrape off any solids first with a spoon — never rub. Then blot any wet residue with a clean cloth.

2

Identify what you're dealing with. Greasy food (butter, oil, salad dressing) → start with Dawn Powerwash. Tomato-based (pasta sauce, ketchup) → OxiClean Max Force straight away. Protein-based (egg, meat) → cold water first, then pretreater. Turmeric → sodium percarbonate soak in hot water, then sunlight.

3

Apply your pretreater and let it sit for 48 hours minimum. Food stains respond dramatically better with time.

4

Wash on warm or hot with a full enzyme detergent and booster. Check before drying — food stains that have been dried in are significantly harder to shift.

💡 Turmeric is in a category of its own. Sodium percarbonate soak + direct sunlight is the most effective treatment. Sunlight acts as a natural bleaching agent on turmeric specifically — leave the damp garment outside and let the sun do its thing.
⚡ Act fast — do not let it dry, do not add heat
1

Blot immediately with a clean cloth — press, don't rub. Soak up as much as you can before it sets. Mom wine night casualty? It happens to the best of us.

2

Rinse from the back with cold water to push the stain out rather than deeper in. Wine is tannin-based — same family as coffee and tea — so cold water and speed are your friends.

3

Apply a little white vinegar directly to the stain. You can also submerge the whole item in vinegar; it won't hurt it. Let it soak for as long as you can, then wash as normal.

4

💡 This is basically the exact same process as coffee — because they're both tannin stains. (Pure wine, like black coffee. No dairy curveball here, thankfully.) On white-only fabric, a spray of hydrogen peroxide can lift what's left — never on colors, it'll bleach them.
✋ Let it dry and harden first — wet slime just smears
1

Resist the urge to wipe. Wet slime smears into a bigger disaster. Let it dry and harden, or speed it up with an ice cube until it's stiff, then scrape off as much as you can with a dull knife or spoon.

2

Rinse under warm water and repeat the vinegar if any slime remains. Most of it should lift and roll off as it loosens.

4

Pretreat any color left behind (slime dye is the real stainer) with OxiClean Max Force, then wash warm with a full enzyme detergent. Check before drying.

💡 The dye in colored slime is often more stubborn than the slime itself. If a colored shadow remains after the slime is gone, treat it like a dye stain — pretreat, wash, repeat, and don't dry until it's clear.
🕐 Usually easy — but don't grind it in
1

Shake or brush off the loose chalk first — outside if you can. Don't rub it into the fabric; you want as much dry powder gone before water touches it. For sidewalk chalk this step alone does most of the work.

2

Rinse from the back with cool water to flush the powder out of the fibers rather than driving it deeper.

3

For chalk paste or oily/dustless chalk (which has a waxy binder), work in a little blue Dawn to cut the grease, then pretreat with OxiClean Max Force. Plain dry chalk usually skips this step.

4

Wash warm with your normal detergent. Check before drying. Chalk is one of the more forgiving stains — it usually comes out in a single wash once the loose powder is off.

💡 Colored chalk can leave a faint dye tint, especially the bright stuff. If you see color after washing, treat it like dye — pretreat and rewash before it ever sees the dryer.
🕐 It's grease + zinc — you're fighting on two fronts
1

Scrape off the excess with a dull knife, an old credit card, or a paper towel — skim, don't grind. Diaper cream is thick and greasy, so get the gooey bulk off before it becomes a stain you're working into the fabric.

2

Apply a small amount of blue Dawn and work it in. A little goes a long way here — too much will suds up your washer. Diaper cream (Desitin, Boudreaux's, etc.) is mostly petroleum and oils, and Dawn is a degreaser, so this is exactly its job. Scrub gently with an old toothbrush and let it sit at least 30 minutes, longer is better.

3

The yellow-grey stain is from zinc oxide. For that part, rubbing alcohol works well — pour or spray it on, rub the fabric together, and rinse under cool water. Repeat until it lightens.

4

Wash warm with a full enzyme detergent and an oxygen bleach booster. The more rinses the better for this stain — make sure your rinse water runs completely clear before you stop. Air dry, and check first: heat sets any grease left behind.

💡 Zinc staining is cosmetic — even if a faint yellow tint lingers, the cream itself is out and it'll keep fading with each wash. Just don't tumble dry until you're happy, because the dryer locks in the greasy part for good.

Scrape off any solid first. If it's fresh melted chocolate, let it harden for a minute before scraping — trying to treat it liquid just smears it further.

Dab blue Dawn dish soap directly onto the stain. Don't rub — dab. Work it in gently with your fingers and let it sit for a few minutes.

Soak in cold water — overnight if you can. This is the dish soap and soak method, and it works. Cold water only. Do not be tempted by warm.

4

Wash as normal with your regular detergent and booster. Check before drying.

🕐 Pretreat before every wash — these get worse over time
1

Why sunscreen stains are their own whole thing. Sunscreen is a cocktail of UV filters (which are yellow — that's not a coincidence), oils, and film-forming polymers that make it water resistant on your skin. Great for the beach. A nightmare for fabric. The higher the SPF, the more UV filters, the worse the staining risk. You're welcome for that information.

2

That orange-rust color? That's the UV filters reacting with copper and iron ions in your tap water during washing, then oxidizing as the fabric dries. Essentially: rust. On your clothes. From sunscreen. Yes, really. This is why hard water makes sunscreen stains significantly worse — more minerals, more reaction, more orange.

3

Pretreat before every single wash — apply liquid detergent directly to any sunscreen-exposed areas and let it sit for at least 30 minutes. Don't skip this and just throw it in the wash — the heat will set the oils and make it worse.

4

For existing yellow or orange stains: the oil component responds to Dawn dish soap first — same as grease. Follow with OxiClean Max Force and let it sit for 48 hours. If your water is hard, add Calgon to the wash to chelate those metal ions before they can react. Wash on warm.

5

If it still persists: soak in hot water with oxygen bleach overnight, then wash as normal. Repeat. Sunscreen stains that have been through the dryer multiple times are genuinely very difficult — manage expectations and keep going.

💡 Prevention is so much easier than treatment here. Wait 20 minutes after applying sunscreen before getting dressed — letting it form a film on your skin first dramatically reduces how much transfers to fabric. Wearing dark colors helps too (sunscreen stains basically disappear on black fabric). And if you're using a Korean sunscreen, good news — they're typically formulated with newer-generation UV filters that are far less likely to cause staining.
🕐 Don't put it in the dryer — heat sets rust permanently
1

Lay the garment flat on a clean surface and saturate the stain with distilled white vinegar. Regular cleaning vinegar works too — you want the acidity to start breaking down the iron oxide. This is rust we're dealing with. Acid is its enemy.

2

Sprinkle a generous layer of salt over the vinegar while it's still wet. The salt acts as a mild abrasive and helps draw the rust out of the fibers. Let the whole thing sit for 30 minutes to an hour — longer if it's a stubborn or old stain.

3

Gently scrub with a soft nylon brush — an old toothbrush works perfectly. Don't go hard on the fabric; you're coaxing the rust out, not sanding the garment down.

4

Rinse thoroughly in cold water and wash as normal. Check before drying — always check before drying. If the stain is still there, repeat the whole process before that dryer gets anywhere near it.

5

If the vinegar and salt method isn't cutting it: Carbona Stain Devil #9 is specifically formulated for rust and is genuinely very effective. Follow the instructions closely — it's a specialty product, not something to freestyle with.

💡 Never use chlorine bleach on rust stains. It reacts with the iron and makes the stain permanently darker. Counterintuitive, deeply annoying, absolutely true.
🕐 The fix depends entirely on what kind of glue it is
1

School glue, washable glue, or Mod Podge (water-based): the easy ones. Scrape off the excess, soak in the hottest water the fabric can handle to soften, then scrape again. Work in blue Dawn (especially for Mod Podge — it's a decoupage glue and Dawn cuts right through it), pretreat with OxiClean Max Force, and wash. Usually comes right out.

2

Hot glue: freeze it. An hour in the freezer makes it brittle enough to peel or chip right off. Pretreat any residue and wash as normal.

3

Super glue (the nightmare): let it dry, scrape off what you can, then dab acetone (plain nail polish remover) on with a cotton swab. Test a hidden seam first — acetone can wreck synthetics and never goes near acetate. Blot, repeat, wash. Check before drying — always.

💡 Real talk: super glue is the one even the pros sometimes can't beat — some stain guides flatly call it unremovable. Acetone gives you a fighting chance, but manage your expectations. School glue, though? Comes out almost every time. Know which battle you're fighting.

Apply liquid dish soap and work it into the stain. Let it stand a few minutes, then rub the fabric under warm water to lift the stain.

Don't throw it out yet. The dryer makes stains harder to remove, not always impossible. A lot of the time you can still save it.

2

🕐 Prevention is everything here — treatment is rough
1

What is a polyquat stain — and why is it so evil? Store-bought bubble solution is loaded with polyquaternium-7, a cationic (positively charged) polymer. Here's the problem: your clothes are negatively charged, so the second the bubble solution hits fabric, the polyquat latches on and bonds instantly — basically invisibly. You won't even see it happen. Then in the wash it acts like a magnet, grabbing every bit of loose dirt and dye floating in the water and turning into a gray, greasy, dingy stain that is genuinely very hard to remove. (It's also in shampoos, leave-in conditioners, and hairsprays, for the same reason.) Do bubble parties shirtless, in swimsuits, or in clothes you don't care about. You've been warned.

2

The hack that prevents this entirely: pour out the store-bought bubble solution. Dissolve 1 tsp of blue Dawn in warm water and refill the bottle. It blows just as good bubbles, it's completely non-toxic, and if it gets on clothes it washes out without a trace. No polyquat. No stain. No heartbreak.

💡 You already have the Dawn — for laundry, for dishes, for grease stains. It does everything. This is just one more thing. Pass this hack on to every toddler mom you know.
⚡ Turmeric especially — do not let this dry if you can help it

2

Apply OxiClean Max Force or your pretreater of choice and let it sit for as long as possible — at least a few hours, overnight if you can. Then wash as normal with a full enzyme detergent and booster.

4

If the stain persists after washing — lay it in the sun. Yes. The sun. Direct sunlight is a natural bleaching agent that specifically targets turmeric pigment in a way that detergent simply cannot. Leave the damp garment outside until dry. It works. It's annoying, but it works.

💡 So maybe try not to use turmeric in the winter, ok? ☀️ And when you do lay it in the sun — make sure it's been fully washed first. Wet is good. Pretreater still on it is not. The sun interacting with pretreater ingredients can cause extra yellowing. Wash, then sun.

Let's get washing

How to Actually Do a Load

You are never washing on cold again. Cold is dead to us. Warm or hot, every time — that's how enzymes and surfactants actually activate. Also: towels ONLY get washed with other towels. This is non-negotiable.

"But cold is better for the environment!" Cool. These are your clothes. Wash them on warm.

1

Add any powdered detergent, boosters, or water softeners directly into the drum — before the clothes go in.

2

Load clothes to 75% capacity. Not 80%. Not "pretty full." 75%.

3

Add 2–4 tbsp of liquid detergent into your machine's detergent section. If you've softened your water correctly, 4 tbsp is the max you ever need. Washing a smaller load? Down-dose your detergent — this amount is for a normal full load. For powder, 2–3 heaping tbsp in the drum. No idea what 3 tbsp looks like? It's about halfway between lines 2 and 3 on a liquid detergent cup. For powders, keep a cheap measuring spoon in the laundry room and thank me later.

4

Add ¼ cup of a liquid rinse aid (DIY or store-bought) OR 1–2 tbsp of dry citric acid crystals into the softener section — whatever your preference. If your machine doesn't have one, use a Downy ball tossed in the drum.

5

Select warm or hot and choose as many extra rinses as your heart desires — but ALWAYS choose at least 1 extra rinse. If you're nervous about dye bleed, toss in a color catcher sheet — reusable until they turn a deep navy color.

6

Walk away for 2 hours. Come back to the cleanest laundry of your entire life.

Why Citric Acid in the Rinse?

If you're using vinegar in your rinse cycle — first of all, how much are you using? Because it takes 2–3 cups of household cleaning vinegar to actually do anything. Citric acid is more concentrated, cheaper, and your clothes won't smell like a salad. We're switching. Right now.

🧫

Neutralizes detergent residue

Detergent is alkaline. Citric acid neutralizes what's left behind — eliminating that stiff, scratchy feeling even if you accidentally overdosed. Which you have. We all have.

💎

Fights hard water minerals

Binds to calcium and magnesium so they rinse away instead of leaving a chalky film on your clothes or slowly destroying your machine from the inside.

🌿

Softens without coating

Fabric softener deposits a waxy layer that traps smells and reduces absorbency over time. Citric acid softens by removing buildup. Nothing added. Just residue gone.

⚙️

Descales your machine

Regular use prevents mineral scale from building up inside the drum, pipes, and heating element. Your machine won't thank you out loud. But it will last longer.

A religion, not a suggestion

Keeping Towels & Sheets Soft and Bright

If your towels feel scratchy and stiff, that's not wear and tear — that's detergent buildup coating the fibers so they can't absorb water properly. The good news: it's completely reversible.

Towels ONLY get washed with other towels. This is not a suggestion. This is a way of life.

🔄 Towel rehab — stripping the buildup
1️⃣

Add ½ cup of citric acid directly to the drum.

2️⃣

Load towels and wash on HOT, heavy soil, with extra rinses. Repeat until you see no more suds — that's all the old detergent coming out. Then promise me you won't overdose your detergent ever again.

3️⃣

Dry on a low/delicate heat setting. Towels are a looped fabric — high heat destroys them. Pull out when barely damp and fold as normal.

🤍 Keeping white towels white

Hot, always. Extra rinses, always. Slightly less detergent than you think — you're clean when you use a towel, so there's genuinely not much dirt for the detergent to grab onto. If they start going dingy, soak in a hot bath with OxiClean White Revive or pure sodium percarbonate for a few hours, then wash and dry as normal. They will come back.

🛏 White sheets — same rules, extra step

Follow the white towel routine, but pretreat any stains first. I have a large golden retriever who sleeps in our bed every single night and our cotton sheets are still a lovely bright white. The routine works. The dog stays.

When the red sock strikes

Dye Bleed & Color Transfer

We've all done it. We're moms, we're tired, just trying to get the laundry done — and a rogue red shirt or sock makes it into the load. Now everything is PINK. While I wish we lived in Barbieland, that is sadly not the case. Here's how to prevent it — and how to fix it.

The good news: dye bleed is almost always fixable, as long as you catch it before the dryer. The dryer is where pink becomes permanent.

Dye bleed happens when loose, unfixed dye escapes from one fabric in the wash and redeposits onto everything else. It's most common with new clothes (especially deep reds, indigos, and dark dyes), hot water (which loosens dye faster), and long soaks. Once that loose dye is floating in the water, it'll grab onto whatever's available — usually your favorite white shirt.

One quiet hero in prevention: anti-redeposition agents. These are ingredients in good detergents that keep loosened dye and soil suspended in the water so they rinse away instead of resettling onto your clothes. Most of the detergents recommended in this guide contain them — it's one more reason the cheap stuff lets you down.

🛡️ Preventing it

Sort by color and wash new dark or bright items separately for the first few washes. Toss a color catcher sheet in with mixed loads — they absorb loose dye before it can transfer, and they're reusable until they come out deep navy. When in doubt, separate it out.

🚑 Fixing a bled load

Do NOT dry it — heat sets the dye permanently. While everything is still wet, rewash the affected items with Carbona Color Run Remover. This stuff saved an ENTIRE load of my newborn's clothes that had turned pink — I'm not exaggerating when I say it works like magic. One quick note: test it on a small, less visible area first to make sure you'll get the result you want.

🔁 If it still won't budge

Repeat the Carbona treatment — sometimes a heavily bled item needs two rounds. For stubborn cases on white-only fabrics, a hot soak with oxygen bleach can help lift what's left. Keep it wet, keep treating, and only dry once the color is fully gone.

⚠️ Little Sleepies moms, READ THIS before you reach for the Carbona. If you're obsessed with your bamboo viscose pajamas (I see you, I know, they're perfect) — here's something I recently learned the hard way: bamboo viscose holds its dye differently than cotton does. That means a dye remover like Carbona Color Run Remover can't always tell the difference between the dye that bled ONTO your pajamas and the factory dye that's supposed to BE there. Translation: run color remover on your pink Little Sleepies and they might come out white, or lose the pink entirely. Always, always test on a hidden spot first — and if it lifts the real color, stop and try gentler dye-bleed methods instead. Bamboo viscose is its own beast.
💡 Already dried it and the dye is set? I won't lie to you — that's a much harder fix, and sometimes it's not reversible at all. Carbona is still worth a shot, but manage expectations. This is exactly why "don't dry it until you've checked" is a rule I will die on.

For the moms on the sideline

I'm a Sports Mom — Help!

Stains and stink are basically a sponsorship deal when you have athletes. Here's how to handle the specific chaos each sport brings.

Whoever decided that white pants belong on a dirt field is literally insane. Anyway. Here's how to undo the damage.

1

Everything into a boiling hot soak — dissolve 1½ cups of powdered detergent in the hot water and submerge jerseys, socks, the whole uniform. Soak for at least 1 hour.

2

Pants get their own soak (separate step, just the pants). Hot water + ½ cup of Out White Bright. Soak for at least 1 hour.

3

Drain the gross soak water and throw everything in the washer. Warm or hot, good detergent, multiple rinses. Add 1–2 tbsp of oxygen bleach to the drum if you want to go all in.

Hockey moms, I am about to become one of you. I have done an unhinged amount of research in preparation. Here's what I've learned.

1

Anything that can go in the washer, wash it. Jerseys, socks, base layers — wash as normal and air dry.

2

Everything else must air out after every single practice. Don't let gear sit in a bag. Ever. Open it up, spread it out, let it breathe.

3

Spray non-washable gear with hypochlorous acid to kill bacteria and odor. Buy an enormous jug and a spray bottle and just go to town.

💡 Hypochlorous acid is safe, non-toxic, and genuinely effective — the same thing used in wound care. A large jug costs almost nothing and lasts forever. Keep it in your gear bag.

13 years of my childhood in a pool and an absolutely unholy amount of money spent on swimsuits (looking at you, Fastskin and LZR Racer) — I have some thoughts. Chlorine will absolutely destroy a suit if you let it sit. Sometimes that's the point. Most of the time it is not.

1

Take it OUT of the bag. Do not leave your towel, suit, and cap festering in your very cool TYR bag after practice. Please, please, please take it all out every single time. A wet suit balled up in a dark bag is a science experiment, not a storage solution.

2

Training suits: rinse in fresh cold water after every single practice, then hang dry. That's it. The rinse is what gets the chlorine out before it can eat the fabric. A note: a chlorine-crumbled suit actually makes great extra drag during a taper before a meet — but you don't want all your suits disintegrating after one season, so rinse the ones you want to keep.

3

Technical race suits — you know, the one you spent $450 on for a single race? Let's take care of that. Rinse it in cold water as immediately as you possibly can after the race, and hang dry.

4

NEVER use anything but water on a tech suit. No detergent, no soap, nothing — any of it will ruin the water-repellent coating that makes the suit fast. You do want to swim fast, don't you?

💡 Never wring out a swimsuit — twisting breaks down the elastic fibers. Gently press the water out and always hang dry, never the dryer. Heat is the enemy of every suit you own.

The big guns

Ammonia — When to Call in the Cavalry

Not an everyday product. But if your husband comes home smelling like a garage, this is your friend.

Mechanics' wives, farmers' wives, anyone married to a person who works with their hands — this one's for you. You're welcome.

Ammonia is an alkaline cleaner with a particular talent for cutting through grease, oil, and heavy grime that regular detergent struggles to shift. It's been used in laundry for decades — it just fell out of fashion when detergents got better. On extremely soiled loads, it still earns its keep.

The key word is extremely. This is not something you're reaching for on a Tuesday with a regular load. It's for the mechanic's work clothes. The farm gear. The mystery-stained situation you've already washed twice and it's only getting worse. Used correctly, it's effective and safe. Used casually on everything, it's unnecessary and will shorten the life of your fabrics.

✅ What it's good for

Heavy grease and oil stains, embedded grime, work clothes that smell like a job site, anything where detergent alone isn't making a dent. Pairs especially well with a Spa Day soak — add it right before you transfer clothes to the machine as your final step.

🚫 The one rule you cannot break

Never mix ammonia with bleach — or with any product containing bleach. This produces toxic chloramine gas. It is not a "don't do this at home" warning. It is a do-not-do-this-anywhere warning. Keep them completely separate, always.

💡 How to use it: Pour about 1 cup of plain liquid ammonia into the liquid bleach slot in your machine drawer — that's exactly what that slot is for. On a top loader, you can also add it directly to the drum after the wash water has filled, before the cycle starts. Wash on warm or hot with an extra rinse. And while we're here: you shouldn't be putting liquid bleach in that slot anyway. We have better options. Consider it ammonia's slot now.

What to buy

Plain, unscented household ammonia — the kind with no added surfactants or cleaning agents. It's usually labeled "clear ammonia" or "pure ammonia." The scented versions and multipurpose formulas have other stuff in them you don't need in your laundry. It costs almost nothing and a bottle lasts forever because you're barely using it.

The resurrection

Spa Day 🧖‍♀️

How to give your stinky, dirty, dead-to-you clothes a second chance at life. This one requires commitment. Clear your afternoon.

This is not regular laundry. This is a full intervention. Put on a podcast. We're going to be here for a while.

What is Spa Day? An extended hot soak for clothes that regular washing just isn't cutting — gym clothes with permanent stink, thrift finds, heavily soiled items, or anything you've quietly given up on and shoved to the back of a drawer. The overnight soak lets enzymes and surfactants work deep into the fibers in a way a 45-minute wash cycle simply cannot.

✨ Credit where it's due: Spa Day was created by u/KismaiAesthetics. I cannot take credit for developing this method — but I am happy to share it with the world. There are many ways you can Spa Day, and I suggest checking out r/laundry for more information. Go give him some love.

Gather your tools

Cooler or plastic storage bin with lid
Wooden or plastic cooking spoon
1-cup measuring cup
Old towel (to weigh clothes down)
Tide Original Powder — easiest option
Oxygen bleach — optional, one scoop in the drum (best for whites & colors)
Cleaning gloves (for sensitive skin)
Fine mesh kitchen strainer
Bucket or smaller bin for transfer
Liquid ammonia (odor nuclear option)
Citric acid (for the rehab rinse)
1

Set up your bin in the bathtub. Fill it ⅓ to ½ with hot tap water.

2

Add 1½ cups of Tide Original Powder per ~6 gallons of water. Stir until all granules are fully dissolved — this takes a few minutes. Use the strainer to fish out any stubborn granules. Undissolved powder can leave spots.

3

Add your clothes. Make sure everything is fully submerged. Lay the old towel on top to keep it all under the water. Put the lid on.

4

Wait 8–12 hours. Overnight works great. Set a reminder alarm so you don't forget about your laundry soup.

5

Rinse each item under warm water. Pull pieces from the laundry soup one at a time and rinse them individually — you can squeeze them gently to help the water run through. This differs from the original Spa Day, but I find that when I skip this step I get an unbelievable amount of suds in my wash. Hey — laundry is all about experimentation. Rinse.

6

Prep your washing machine before you transfer. Pre-select all your settings and add your detergent — liquid to the drawer, or powder to the drum. You don't need a lot here: 1 tablespoon of powder or 2 tablespoons of liquid is plenty.

7

Optional — add one or two scoops of oxygen bleach to the drum. This step is best for whites and colors; if you're soaking darks, you can skip it (a brightening oxygen bleach can fade them over time). My personal favorite is 365 Oxygen Whitener. Try not to let it touch the wet clothes directly; contact with wet clothes activates the oxygen bleach early and can cause spotting.

8

Transfer clothes to the machine. If using ammonia, pour 1 cup directly onto the soaked clothes, then immediately close the door and press start. Speed matters here — ammonia should be your very last step.

9

Wash on warm or hot, heavy soil, with an extra rinse. Normal cycles are fine for most fabrics.

10

Dry as normal — delicates hang to dry, everything else on low/delicate heat. Don't dry on hot if you're still working on a stain.

💜 Long-term maintenance: incorporate a lipase detergent or booster into your regular routine to prevent buildup from coming back. Citric acid in every rinse keeps things fresh.

For the furry little stain machines we love

Pet Odors & Accidents

Pet pee is a whole different animal from a regular stain (pun fully intended). Get the chemistry right and the smell actually leaves. Get it wrong and it comes roaring back the second it gets humid — like it was never gone at all.

That smell that keeps coming back? Not your imagination. It's chemistry. And the "cleaner" half of people reach for actually makes it worse.

Here's why pet urine is tricky: fresh urine has a protein component that protease (the enzyme in OxiClean Max Force and most of my booster picks) breaks down beautifully. But as urine ages, it leaves behind uric acid crystals — and those aren't proteins, so regular detergent and even oxygen bleach don't fully break them down. They just sit in the fibers, and every time it gets humid, they reactivate and smell again. That's the comeback funk. That's why you need a cleaner built specifically for it.

🚫 Skip the ammonia — seriously

Never clean pet messes with anything ammonia-based. Urine already smells like ammonia to your pet, so cleaning with more of it basically tells them "yep, this is the bathroom" — and they'll re-mark the exact same spot. (And the universal rule: never mix ammonia with bleach. Toxic gas. Don't.)

🧪 Use the right enzyme

For a fresh accident, OxiClean Max Force and a good enzyme wash handle the protein side and the general funk. For set-in, comes-back-with-humidity urine, reach for a dedicated pet enzyme cleaner built to break down uric acid — I use Nature's Miracle, added right into the wash with your detergent (¼–½ cup per load, per the bottle).

🧖 Spa Day works — with one change

For REALLY soiled pet items, a Spa Day ↑ soak does wonders. Follow every step exactly — except skip the ammonia. On pet stuff, ammonia is the one ingredient you leave out, for all the reasons above. Everything else stays the same.

🐾 From me and my (very large, occasionally very fragrant) golden retriever: hypochlorous acid is a real antimicrobial — gentle, non-toxic, the same stuff used in wound care. I keep a spray bottle by his bed and spritz it down a couple times a week. In my house it genuinely helps keep the everyday dog funk from setting up camp between proper washes. And if your dog has ever had anal gland… situations… iykyk. Here's the real win though: I am already washing an absolutely unhinged amount of laundry for my family. If a couple spritzes a week means I can wash the dog's bed less often — even though I love him with my entire heart — that is a genuine laundry victory and I will take it. It's cheap, it's already in my arsenal, and it buys me time. Sold.

For when you just need to start doing laundry correctly right now. You've got kids. I get it.

  • 1

  • 2

  • 3

  • 5

  • 6

  • 7

  • 8

  • 9

💜 Feeling overwhelmed? Start here.

Deep breath. You do not need a cabinet full of products. If you're staring down your laundry room wondering where to even begin, these are the only things you actually need:

  1. A good detergent — powder or liquid, whatever works for your family. No wrong answer here.
  2. Oxygen bleach powder — OxiClean, 365 Oxygen Whitener, anything with sodium percarbonate in it.
  3. An enzymatic pretreater — for when a stain needs backup before the wash.
  4. A citric acid–based rinse aid — straight citric acid or a ready-made bottle, your call.
  5. A laundry booster — BIZ, FEBU, or DadMode. This is the upgrade that does the heavy lifting.
  6. A water softener — only if you have hard water. If you don't, skip it entirely.

That's it. That's the whole starter kit. Everything else in this guide is a nice-to-have for specific situations — but these six get you actually clean laundry. You've got this. 💪
